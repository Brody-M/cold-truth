# Springfield Three — Case Risk Notes

**Assessment date:** 2026-07-10  
**Overall risk:** Moderate–high, manageable only with a rigid official-source boundary

## Risk review

### Reputational and misinformation risk

The case has accumulated decades of repeated scene details, purported tips, suspect narratives, psychic claims, and online theories. Some have become so familiar that they can be mistaken for settled evidence. The episode must distinguish official facts from attributed local reporting and must not “complete” gaps with commonly repeated lore.

Required limits:

- Do not state that the three women were abducted, left together, or were killed; the public record in this ledger does not establish any of those outcomes.
- Describe the FBI’s approximately 2:15–7:30 a.m. window as a disappearance window, not a confirmed time of a crime.
- Attribute the cars, belongings, and lack of apparent struggle to established reporting or investigators. “No apparent signs” must never become “nothing was disturbed” or proof of a particular scenario.
- Do not include the broken porch-light globe, erased answering-machine message, suspicious van, letters, calls, parking-structure rumor, or map claims under the current source boundary.
- Recheck active-case status directly with Springfield Police immediately before publication.

### Legal and suspect-claim risk

Naming or indirectly identifying a suspect would create substantial defamation and fairness risk because the approved ledger contains no law-enforcement-corroborated suspect claim, charging document, or court finding.

Required limits:

- Name no suspect, person of interest, convicted offender, alleged confessor, or relative as potentially responsible.
- Do not use proximity, criminal history, failed polygraphs, reported confessions, or online accusation as evidence.
- Do not imply that a public tip or searched location was credible merely because authorities examined it.
- Do not assign fault to investigators without primary documentation and a meaningful opportunity for official response.

### Victim-family and sensitivity risk

Three people and multiple families are involved, and the absence of a confirmed outcome is itself central to the harm.

Required limits:

- Use the women’s names consistently and avoid reducing them to a collective mystery label after orientation.
- Avoid invented personality profiles, imagined dialogue, dramatized final moments, or claims about how family members felt beyond attributed public statements.
- Do not describe the women as deceased unless supported by a specific legal record and clearly framed as a legal status, not a factual finding about what happened.
- Use family remembrance sparingly and do not exploit private health or grief details.

### Narrative and audience risk

The quiet residence, missing belongings context, and unknown outcome can support calm narration, but the topic invites sensational theory stacking. A source-led episode should use chronology and investigative scale as its engine, not ominous speculation.

## Facts requiring extra corroboration before use

- Any scene detail beyond SP-06 and SP-07.
- Any specific tip, vehicle, caller, letter, map, confession, suspect, or searched site.
- Claims about evidence contamination or the conduct of visitors to the residence.
- Any statement that the women were legally declared dead.
- Current reward amounts, current tip lines, or present agency assignments not documented in the accepted ledger.
- Claims about forensic testing, physical evidence, or a DNA profile; none is approved in the current ledger.

## Format suitability

**Appropriate with restrictions.** The case can fit Cold Truth’s calm, reliable style for women 25–45 and background listeners because the verified chronology, three-person relationship context, search response, and continuing active status form a coherent narrative. Suitability depends on excluding the case’s large theory ecosystem and avoiding sensational treatment of the home or the families’ uncertainty.
