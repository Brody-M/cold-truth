HOOK
On the night of February 13th, 2000, a nine-year-old girl in Shelby, North Carolina packed a backpack, walked out of her house, and disappeared into a storm.
No one took her. No one called her. No one was waiting at the door.
She left on her own.
That detail has never been explained. In twenty-four years, investigators have interviewed hundreds of people, followed leads across multiple states, and never found Asha Degree. What they did find, buried in a bag on the side of a highway, raised more questions than it answered.
This is what we know. This is what we don't. And this is why the case is still open today.
[B-ROLL: small town street at night, wet pavement reflecting streetlights]
CASE INTRO
[B-ROLL: quiet suburban neighborhood, modest ranch-style homes, daytime, green lawns]
Asha Degree was nine years old and in the fourth grade at Fallston Elementary School in Shelby, North Carolina. By every account from her family, her teachers, and the people who knew her, she was a cautious, quiet child. Not the kind of kid who pushed limits or snuck out of the house. Not the kind of kid who wandered.
She lived with her parents, Harold and Iquilla Degree, and her older brother O'Bryant in a comfortable home in Cleveland County. Her father worked. Her mother was involved in her schooling. The family attended church. Asha was described as shy, bookish, and close to her family.
[B-ROLL: elementary school exterior, yellow school bus, children's drawings on classroom wall]
She liked books. She liked school. Her teachers described her as a good student who rarely caused problems and stayed close to people she trusted.
February 13th, 2000, was a Sunday. The next day was Valentine's Day. Asha and her brother O'Bryant had spent the evening watching the NBA All-Star Game with their father. It was storming outside. North Carolina was getting heavy rain and strong winds. The family went to bed around nine-thirty in the evening.
Nothing, by any account, was wrong.
[B-ROLL: living room television glow, rain hitting a window at night]
THE INCIDENT
[B-ROLL: clock on a nightstand showing 2:30 AM, dark bedroom]
Sometime between 2:30 and 4:00 in the morning, Asha Degree got out of bed.
She packed a backpack. Witnesses would later tell investigators it appeared to contain several items, including pencils, markers, a book, and clothing. She got dressed. She walked out of the house.
Her parents did not hear her leave.
[B-ROLL: rural highway at night, no cars, just road and trees, rain on the lens]
What happened next is built almost entirely from two witness accounts, because that highway in the early hours of February 14th was not completely empty.
A driver traveling on Highway 74 reported seeing a young girl walking along the road sometime around 3:45 in the morning. The driver was startled enough to slow down. Then he turned around and came back. When he returned to the spot where he had seen her, she was gone. She had ducked into the woods off the side of the road.
A second driver reported a similar sighting in the same area around the same time.
Two separate people saw a small child walking alone on a dark highway in a rainstorm before 4:00 in the morning. Both times, when they got closer or turned back, she hid from them.
[B-ROLL: tree line at the edge of a rural highway, dense brush, early morning fog]
She was hiding from people who might have helped her.
That detail is important. Investigators noted it early. Asha was not disoriented. She was not running from something in a panic. She appeared to be going somewhere deliberately, and she did not want to be stopped.
Her family reported her missing the next morning when they found her bed empty. She had not arrived at school. She had not called anyone. She was simply gone.
[B-ROLL: school hallway, empty lockers, morning light through windows]
THE INVESTIGATION
[B-ROLL: law enforcement vehicles parked along a rural road, officers in the tree line]
The search began immediately. Volunteers combed the woods along Highway 74. The FBI was brought in. Investigators interviewed family, neighbors, teachers, church members, and classmates. They searched the area where the witnesses had seen her walking. They searched fields, streams, and abandoned structures in the surrounding area.
They found nothing.
[B-ROLL: empty field at sunrise, search dogs, yellow police tape in the distance]
For investigators, the first and most persistent question was the one that still has no answer: why did she leave?
There was no obvious reason. Her parents had not reported any conflict or disturbance in the home. Her teachers had not noticed any change in her behavior. Her classmates did not report that she had spoken about running away. There were no threats, no known predators, no person in her life who had made advances or expressed unusual interest in her.
But investigators did note one thing. In the weeks before she disappeared, Asha's teacher had observed a subtle shift. She seemed quieter than usual. A little withdrawn. It was not alarming enough to flag formally, but it was there.
[B-ROLL: handwritten notes on a desk, a teacher's gradebook, classroom chairs stacked at the end of the day]
Whether that had anything to do with why she left has never been established.
The witness accounts placed her moving west on Highway 74. That direction leads away from Shelby and toward the smaller communities and rural stretches of Cleveland County. Investigators followed that path and found no physical evidence of where she went after she entered the tree line.
Then, fourteen months later, something changed.
[B-ROLL: highway construction crew, orange cones, workers digging near a tree line]
In June of 2001, a construction crew was working about a mile and a half from where Asha had been seen on Highway 74. They were clearing land near a shed on the property of a business off the highway. While working, they found a plastic bag buried in the ground.
Inside the bag was a backpack. Inside the backpack were Asha's belongings.
[B-ROLL: close-up of a plastic evidence bag, rubber-gloved hands, a folding table with items laid out]
Her pencils. Her markers. Her clothing. Items her family confirmed belonged to her.
The bag had been deliberately buried. Someone had wrapped her belongings, placed them in the ground, and covered them. This was not a backpack dropped or thrown from a car. Someone had taken the time to bury it.
That location was approximately a mile and a half from where the witnesses had seen her walking. It was near a shed. It was off the main road.
Asha was not found with the bag. Her remains have never been located.
[B-ROLL: aerial view of rural North Carolina, patchwork fields and tree lines, two-lane roads]
The buried backpack shifted the investigation. It confirmed that someone else had been involved at some point, even if investigators could not determine exactly when or how. It also suggested that whoever buried it knew the area well enough to choose a concealed spot that went undiscovered for over a year.
TWISTS AND TURNING POINTS
[B-ROLL: a computer screen showing a news website, hands typing, news headline visible but not readable]
The case drew national attention in waves over the years, each time a development surfaced and then went quiet again.
In 2019, the FBI released new images of items found in Asha's backpack, including a yellow pencil with a specific design and a Valentine's Day eraser. The release was a direct appeal to the public. Someone made those items. Someone sold them. Someone may remember them.
The release generated significant media coverage and a new round of tips. None led to a public break in the case.
[B-ROLL: FBI building exterior, American flag, news cameras on a sidewalk]
In 2024, the FBI confirmed that the investigation remained active and that agents were still pursuing leads. The agency did not disclose the specific nature of those leads, which is standard practice in active investigations. What the statement confirmed is that after twenty-four years, the case has not gone cold in the eyes of the people working it.
There is still someone, or something, they are looking for.
[B-ROLL: calendar pages, years changing, a child's bedroom with the light on]
One detail that has stayed with investigators and with the family across all these years is the simplest and hardest one to answer. Asha Degree left home voluntarily. She was nine years old. She packed deliberately, she dressed, she walked out, and she hid from people who tried to approach her.
That means she had a reason. She had a destination, or at least a direction. She had decided, on that stormy night, that she needed to go somewhere.
No one knows where. No one knows why.
RESOLUTION AND CURRENT STATUS
[B-ROLL: a mother sitting at a kitchen table, hands folded, window light, face not shown]
Harold and Iquilla Degree have never stopped speaking publicly about their daughter. They have participated in anniversary coverage, spoken to journalists, and worked with investigators for more than two decades. They have asked, consistently and without bitterness, for anyone who knows anything to come forward.
Asha would be thirty-three years old today.
[B-ROLL: a child's sneakers on a doorstep, a school photo frame on a mantle, a backpack hanging on a hook by a door]
The FBI's tip line for the Asha Degree case remains open. The case number is 1-800-CALL-FBI. Her case is listed on the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children database. Cleveland County Sheriff's Office and the FBI Charlotte Division continue to list it as active.
In the twenty-four years since that Valentine's Day morning, no one has been charged. No confirmed sighting has been made after those two witnesses on Highway 74. No explanation has been given for why she left.
What remains is a family that has waited more than two decades for an answer, a community that still remembers, and a nine-year-old girl who packed her bag in a storm and walked out into the dark for a reason nobody has been able to name.
[B-ROLL: Highway 74 at dawn, light starting to come over the tree line, empty road stretching ahead]
If you grew up in Cleveland County, if you knew Asha or her family, if you remember something from that February or the months around it, the FBI wants to hear from you. You can submit a tip at tips.fbi.gov or call 1-800-CALL-FBI. You can also contact the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children at 1-800-THE-LOST.
Twenty-four years is a long time to carry something. If you know something, now is the time.
CLOSING CTA
[B-ROLL: a quiet road at sunrise, birds, still air after a storm]
If this case is new to you, I hope it stays with you the way it has stayed with me. Cases like Asha's are exactly why this channel exists. The ones that don't get the coverage they deserve. The ones where a family is still waiting.
I'll see you next time on Cold Truth.
