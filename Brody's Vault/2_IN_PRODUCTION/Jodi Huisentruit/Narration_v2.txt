At roughly 4:10 in the morning, a colleague called Jodi Huisentruit. The KIMT morning anchor had not arrived for work.

Before the day was fully underway, attention had shifted to the parking lot beside Jodi's car. Public reporting would describe drag marks and personal belongings left behind.

More than thirty years later, those details still do not explain what happened to Jodi.

Jodi worked as a morning anchor for KIMT in Mason City, Iowa. On June 27, 1995, she missed her early shift.

Morning television begins long before most viewers start their day. Jodi was expected at the station early enough to prepare for a live broadcast, which is why her absence was noticed before sunrise. That context explains the urgency at work; it does not explain her disappearance.

This account stays inside a narrow boundary: the documented timeline, the publicly reported evidence, and official updates from investigators. Theories may fill the silence around a missing-person case, but they are not the same as verified facts.

That distinction matters here. The available records establish several points in time. They do not establish why Jodi disappeared or where she went.

The public timeline begins with the early-morning call. A colleague contacted Jodi at roughly 4:10 a.m. When she still had not arrived, someone at the station requested a welfare check. The dispatch record shows police were sent to her apartment complex at 7:13 a.m.

That record gives the official response a verifiable time. It does not tell us what happened before police arrived, or what they expected to find when the request was sent.

Reporting later described drag marks beside Jodi's car. It also listed a bent key for her Miata, a red pair of heels, earrings, a blow dryer, and hair spray in the parking lot.

Those details document what was reportedly found. They do not identify who was responsible, establish a motive, or show where Jodi went after leaving home for work.

Iowa's Missing Person Information Clearinghouse continues to list Jodi and directs anyone with information to Mason City Police or the Iowa Division of Criminal Investigation.

Investigators have continued to pursue leads. In November 2025, Mason City Police searched an area in Winsted, Minnesota. The department said the search was part of the investigation rather than the result of a new tip. Nothing significant was found.

A separate public update concerned a 2017 search warrant seeking GPS data connected to two vehicles. The warrant was linked to John Vansice's vehicles and was partly unsealed in 2025, but its supporting affidavit remained sealed because the case was ongoing.

The dates matter. The request for location data was made years before part of the warrant became public. Even after that partial release, the material explaining why investigators sought it was still unavailable for public review.

That connection does not establish guilt. A sealed affidavit cannot be treated as public proof, and its contents cannot responsibly be used to fill the unanswered parts of the case.

Together, those updates show two different investigative steps: a physical search and a legal effort to obtain location data. Neither produced a public resolution. A search, a warrant, or a named connection may matter without proving what happened.

They also show how an old investigation can continue without giving the public a complete picture. Confirmed activity is not the same as a confirmed explanation.

There is no resolution to offer.

At the 31st anniversary of Jodi's disappearance in June 2026, the lead investigator said the case remained active. That status has a limited meaning: the case has not been closed, and investigators are still asking for information. It does not signal a new breakthrough or promise an answer.

For the people who knew Jodi, the central fact has not changed. She is still missing.

The record leaves us with times on a clock, a parking lot, and belongings that were never meant to become evidence. Everything after that remains unknown.
